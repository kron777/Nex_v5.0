## C. The outstanding queue

### C1 The scorer — the smallest label set that settles it

**One number: 120 labels.** Sampling rule, and why it is not uniform:

| score band | share of 14 d | value of a label there |
|---|---|---|
| [0.0, 0.2) | **57.0%** | ~none — no weight change flips these |
| [0.2, 0.3) | 11.9% | low |
| **[0.3, 0.5)** | **15.0%** | **high — decision band** |
| **[0.5, 0.7)** | **10.0%** | **high — decision band** |
| [0.7, 1.0) | 6.0% | anchors only |

**Rule:** 80 sampled uniformly at random from the decision band **[0.3, 0.7)** — 1,787 fires available over 14 d, 128/day, so no scarcity — plus **40 anchors**, 20 from [0.8, 1.0) and 20 from [0.0, 0.2), to catch gross miscalibration. Label binary striking/ordinary. Stratify across days so a single day's topic cannot dominate.

**Why not R28's 150–250:** uniform sampling spends 57% of the budget in [0.0, 0.2) where nothing can flip. Concentrating on the band buys the same information for half the labels.

**What an hour of your time actually buys — the honest version.** At 15–20 s per label (read a ~340-char fire, one judgement), an hour is 180–240 labels. **120 labels is ~35 minutes.** With 80 in-band labels the 95% CI on band accuracy is about ±0.11 — enough to answer *"is the scorer near-random at its own decision boundary?"*, which is the question that decides whether a refit is worth doing.

**What it does NOT buy: the refit itself.** 120 labels settles *whether*; fitting five weights with any stability needs several hundred, and the existing 103-example fit with `fn:0` is the cautionary example. **One hour buys a decision, not a model.**
